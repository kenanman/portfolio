Green Wave: Cleaning Up the Traffic We Can't Eliminate

A policy-and-technology proposal to cut traffic emissions and air pollution in the UAE, prepared and pitched in a 2-hour hackathon.

Format2-Hour Hackathon Build

1. The Challenge

Traffic congestion contributes heavily to air pollution and greenhouse gas emissions in the UAE. Our hackathon challenge was straightforward: how can the UAE put cleaner transport systems in place to reduce the environmental footprint of traffic?

Two main insights shaped our proposal:

  • Adding more highway lanes doesn't work. Due to induced demand, widening roads quickly attracts more cars and does nothing to lower emissions.
  • The goal is cutting emissions, not just vehicle speed. Even if congestion clears up, petrol cars still pollute. We wanted to attack the emissions directly.
  • Our proposal addressed five UN Sustainable Development Goals: SDG 3SDG 9SDG 11SDG 12SDG 13

Stop-and-go driving burns fuel without moving anyone forward, making congested traffic especially dirty per kilometer. Meanwhile, electric cars were only around 6% of new car sales in 2024, and cheap petrol makes driving the default choice for most people.

3. The Main Idea

We can't get rid of all traffic overnight, but we can make trips far cleaner. Instead of asking "how do we speed up cars?", we asked "how do we make clean transport the easiest, cheapest choice?"

4. The Solution: Three Pillars

1. Electrify Fleets

  • Subsidize EV purchases to compete with petrol cars
  • Expand public chargers at malls, fuel stations, and apartment buildings
  • Transition public bus fleets to electric on major routes

2. Smart Traffic Flow

  • AI-adaptive traffic lights to reduce idling at empty red lights
  • Dynamic emission tolls: zero-emission cars pass free, heavy polluters pay more at peak hours
  • Live roadside air quality monitors displayed on a public dashboard

3. Shift & Share

  • Free public transit (metro, buses, trams) funded directly by toll and polluter fees
  • A single smartphone app combining metro, bus, taxi, and e-scooters
  • Employer commuter programs (shuttles and staggered working hours)

The Core Differentiator: Free Transit Funded by Polluters

Once someone buys a car, each trip feels "free", while public transit costs money every single time you board. Making buses and metros free while charging peak-hour tolls on polluting vehicles flips that calculation. It works on existing infrastructure without waiting years for new rail lines to be built.

Rough Impact Estimate

If 250,000 to 500,000 daily commuters switch from cars to public transit, and each car accounts for roughly 2.3 tonnes of CO₂ a year, this policy alone could avoid approximately 0.6 to 1.2 million tonnes of CO₂ annually.

6. Design Choices: Ideas We Dropped

Flying electric taxis (eVTOL): Dropped. We brainstormed flying taxis early on because they sounded futuristic. But in reality, they are extremely expensive, carry only a few wealthy passengers, and do nothing to move thousands of everyday commuters. We dropped the gimmick to focus on real solutions: buses and trains.
Adding more highway lanes: Rejected. Widening roads just attracts more traffic within a year or two (induced demand), increasing emissions instead of lowering them.

7. Pitching to the Judges

We boiled our 20-slide research deck down to a focused 8-slide, 5-minute presentation for the AUS faculty jury:

  1. The real problem: emissions and idle time, not just vehicle speed
  2. The 3-pillar framework: Electrify, Smart Flow, Shift & Share
  3. Our key differentiator: free transit funded by polluter tolls
  4. Five SDGs addressed on one clear slide
  5. Projected 2030 impact and realistic rollout stages
  6. Closing call to action and Q&A defense

8. What I Learned as Team Leader

  • Focus on the real problem: Shifting our goal from "moving cars faster" to "cutting emissions" completely changed which ideas were worth keeping.
  • Drop flashy gimmicks early: Choosing buses and free transit over flying taxis made our pitch much more grounded and respected by the judges.
  • A 5-minute timer forces clarity: You can't explain ten complicated things. You need one solid idea, the data to back it up, and a realistic way to pay for it.
