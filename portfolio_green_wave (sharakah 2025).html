1. The Challenge

Heavy traffic congestion contributes to air pollution and greenhouse gas emissions in the UAE, harming the environment and human health. The brief: how can the UAE put cleaner transportation systems in place to reduce the environmental impact of traffic congestion?

Two constraints shaped everything:

  • Adding lanes is not an answer. It may ease congestion briefly, but it attracts more cars and does nothing for emissions.
  • The target is emissions, not just speed. Fixing congestion as well would be a bonus, but the goal is reducing the environmental footprint of traffic.
  • The solution had to address five UN Sustainable Development Goals: SDG 3SDG 9SDG 11SDG 12SDG 13

Idling and stop-start driving burn fuel without moving anyone anywhere, so congested traffic is disproportionately dirty per kilometre. Meanwhile the UAE's EV share of new car sales was only around 6% in 2024, far from its long-term electrification targets, and car ownership is reinforced by cheap fuel and limited transit coverage in some emirates.

3. Key Insight

We can't eliminate traffic overnight, but we can eliminate most of its environmental harm. Instead of asking "how do we move cars faster?", we asked "how do we make every trip cleaner, and make the clean option the easy one?"

4. The Solution: Green Wave

Three strategies that reinforce each other: change what is on the road, change how it flows, and change why people drive.

1. Electrify Everything

  • Stronger EV purchase incentives (benchmarked against Qatar's much larger subsidies)
  • Public charging scaled up fast: malls, fuel stations, residential buildings, solar-powered hubs
  • Electric public bus fleet, prioritising the highest-traffic routes
  • Corporate fleet tax incentives

2. Smart Flow

  • AI adaptive traffic signals to cut idling and stop-start emissions
  • Dynamic, emission-based tolling: zero-emission vehicles pass free, high-emission vehicles pay more at peak
  • Live air-quality sensing at major corridors, shown on a public dashboard

3. Shift & Share

  • Free public transit (metro, bus, tram) funded by toll and emission-fee revenue
  • One mobility app for metro, bus, taxi, e-scooter and bike, with rewards for clean trips
  • Employer commuter programs (shuttles, transit allowances, staggered hours)

The headline idea: free public transit, paid for by polluters

Driving feels free once you own a car; transit feels like a cost on every trip. Making transit free and funding it with revenue from tolls and high-emission vehicles flips that economic signal. It also works immediately on existing infrastructure, benefits lower-income workers the most, and doesn't need years of construction before it has an effect.

Illustrative impact of free transit

If between 250,000 and 500,000 daily car commuters shift to transit, and each removes roughly 2.3 tonnes of CO₂ per year (an assumption; UAE per-car figures may be higher), that is approximately 0.6 to 1.2 million tonnes of CO₂ avoided per year from this one policy.

This is a back-of-envelope scenario, not a model. Real uptake depends on service quality, and international evidence on free transit is mixed: ridership gains are real in several cities, but some of the new riders switch from walking or cycling rather than from cars. That's why the toll and pricing side of the package matters.

7. Projected Outcomes (Team Estimates)

  • Substantial reduction in transport-sector emissions by 2030 (team target: 30–35%)
  • Meaningful drop in traffic-related PM2.5, moving toward WHO guidelines over a longer horizon
  • Healthcare and productivity savings that offset a large share of program cost over time
  • Tens of thousands of green-economy jobs (charging, manufacturing, recycling, software)

These are target-setting projections from the hackathon, not outputs of a validated model. Costs, funding sources (green bonds, toll revenue, public-private partnerships, carbon credits) and benefit figures would need proper economic modeling in a real proposal.

8. Design Decisions: What We Cut and Why

Flying electric taxis (eVTOL), cut. They were in an early draft as a "wow" idea. On review: very high cost per trip, serving only wealthy riders; real safety and regulatory hurdles; high energy use per passenger; and negligible impact on mass-commute emissions. A creative idea that can't scale to the average commuter doesn't solve this problem.
More lanes, rejected. Induced demand means added capacity fills up with more cars, so total emissions rise even if congestion eases briefly.

The replacement, free public transit, was chosen because it is equitable, immediate, cheap to start, and aimed at the biggest source of emissions: everyday commuting.

9. Feasibility Check

  • Technical: EVs, charging, AI signals, tolling and transit apps are all mature technologies already deployed elsewhere.
  • Financial: The UAE has existing toll systems (e.g. Salik in Dubai, Darb in Abu Dhabi) that can be repurposed into emission-based pricing and fund free transit.
  • Policy: Fits the UAE's Net Zero 2050 commitment and post-COP28 positioning.
  • Social: A tech-forward population and strong existing metro adoption in Dubai.
  • Main risks: Transit capacity (free fares only work if trains and buses can absorb new riders), uneven coverage in some emirates, grid load from mass EV charging, and public acceptance of tolling.

10. The Pitch

The solution was condensed from a 20-slide deck into an 8-slide, 5-minute presentation:

  1. Title and hook
  2. The real problem: emissions, not just traffic
  3. The Green Wave: Electrify, Smart Flow, Shift & Share
  4. Free public transit as the creative differentiator
  5. All five SDGs on one slide
  6. Impact by 2030
  7. Realistic timeline
  8. Closing and call to action

Cost breakdowns, governance, and alternatives comparison were moved to backup slides for Q&A.

11. What I Learned

  • Scoping to the real objective (emissions, not speed) changed which ideas were worth keeping.
  • Pressure-testing a flashy idea early, then replacing it with a less glamorous but scalable one, made the proposal stronger.
  • Five minutes forces ruthless prioritisation: one core framework, one memorable idea, one clear call to action.
