ABOUT ME • MECHANICAL & MECHATRONICS ENGINEERING

Who I Am & How I Build

How I got into hardware, what my workspace looks like, and what I've learned from building and debugging physical systems.

1. How I Got Into Hardware

I first got interested in electronics watching my older brother wire up an Arduino kit for school. In Grade 2, I made my first Scratch sprite move in the computer lab, and in Grade 3 I won my school's Typing Master competition — even after arriving halfway through because I had to run back to class for my glasses. In Grade 8, I built a complete 3D game from scratch in RPG in a Box for the 2022 IT Exhibition and published it on itch.io, where it reached around 400 views and 90 downloads.

Because my school had limited lab equipment, I started gathering components and learning on my own. With how my ADHD works, I learn much better by physically building and testing mechanisms with my hands than sitting through pure theory. I write code and use CAD whenever a project requires it — and I've built three desktop PCs from scratch — but mechanical systems, electronics, and hands-on hardware are what I enjoy most.

2. My Workspace, Salvaged Parts & 3D Printer Rebuild

I build all of my projects either at my bedroom desk or in our school's chemistry lab (where my chemistry teacher gives me a pass to work and films test runs for the school's social media). My everyday toolkit is simple: an electric screwdriver set, wire strippers, solder paste, and a basic 15 AED soldering iron. To stay on a high-school budget, I salvage parts wherever I can — recovering four working official Arduino Unos from retired student projects (with my design teacher's permission), dismantling my worn-out X-Rocker Monsoon 4.1 gaming chair for its steel support frame, subwoofer, speakers, tweeters, and RGB control board, and keeping a drawer stocked with Arduino/ESP32 sensors, magnesium strips, homemade ferric oxide, and lithium safely recovered from batteries for chemistry experiments.

My 3D printer is a boxed Voxelab Aquila I bought at a liquidation sale for 300 AED and rebuilt almost completely: new extruder, hotend, PEI magnetic build plate, power supply, Bowden tube, 3D Touch auto-leveling sensor, 3D-printed dampening feet and tool holder, and upgraded cooling fans. When the replacement fan I ordered turned out to be too large for the stock carriage, I took an existing printhead CAD model, modified the geometry to fit the oversized fan, and printed my own custom shroud. Later, when building OzoCore, its 25×25 cm base was too big for the Aquila's 220×220 mm bed—so I spent 30+ hours in Tinkercad modeling 15 custom parts from scratch, including an interlocking 4-quadrant chassis and a symmetrical dual rack-and-pinion spark gap mechanism.

3. Hardware Failures & Competition Troubleshooting

Building OzoCore and the EEZYbotARM MK2 involved plenty of trial and error. I went through roughly 250g of PLA on failed test prints, damaged an OLED display while trying to resolder a tiny SMD capacitor to change its I²C address, and burned out a dust sensor by accidentally connecting it to 5V. On the robotic arm, the gripper micro-servo stripped its gears three times — and I lost 60 AED on servos advertised as "metal gear" that actually had plastic internal gears — before buying genuine 80 AED metal-gear servos that held up under load. The OzoCore build also turned out surprisingly sturdy: right before the competition, the entire assembled unit took an accidental fall from two meters high, and survived with zero damage—no cracks in the 3D-printed chassis, no broken acrylic, and all wiring and electronics intact.

My toughest moment happened on Day 2 of the OzoCore exhibition: while I was away from the booth, someone tampered with the setup and damaged my 12V sealed lead-acid (SLA) battery. Normal use drops a 12V SLA battery to around 11V, but mine read 2V — meaning the internal cells were dead. I noticed it right as the judges arrived, immediately bypassed the battery, and wired OzoCore directly to a bench power supply on the spot so the live demonstration ran without a hitch.

4. Outside the Lab & What I Want to Build Next

Typing & Languages: Ever since a 1st-grade teacher asked us to type our names, I've loved doing things fast. I won 2nd place at a Sharaf DG typing competition (winning a Huawei tablet, missing 1st place by 1 WPM due to long number strings) and now reach 243 WPM peak (226 WPM at 100% accuracy). Alongside native Arabic and English, I'm studying Japanese — which I started with a friend and continue with the goal of studying engineering in Japan.

Running & Sports: Outside of hardware, I run middle-distance and 5Ks (2:28 800m PR while carrying a 250g phone; sub-22:30 5K on my second attempt), play badminton with a friend at school for fun (which has also helped him get in shape), play table tennis, and hunt liquidation sales for running shoes (picking up Grade-B Nike Vaporfly 4 Jakob editions and On Cloud X for 70 AED each).

Next Builds: In a university lab, I want to move beyond 3D-printed hobby arms to build a rigid metal robotic arm, an improved OzoCore V2, and mechanical/cryogenic systems (after researching how to build a small-scale liquid nitrogen generator from scratch for my uncle).

PROJECT 01 // HIGH-VOLTAGE HARDWARE • 25kV AC (6–15V 65W) DRIVER + 30kV CW MULTIPLIER

OzoCore: 250kV High-Voltage Ozone Generator

25kV AC (6–15V / 65W Max) 30kV-Rated: 15 Caps + 20 Diodes Rack & Pinion Adjustable Electrodes Full OzoCore Technical Page

Corona-Discharge Ozone Generation & Mechanical Electrode Control

OzoCore is a self-contained high-voltage ozone generator built inside a sealed 25 × 25 × 25 cm (15.625 L) clear acrylic chamber. A front PWM voltage knob (6.0 V to 15.0 V) controls a 25 kV AC high-frequency driver (65 W max), which feeds a custom Cockcroft-Walton voltage multiplier assembled from 15 ceramic capacitors and 20 high-voltage diodes (rated at 30 kV). The multiplier is potted in clear epoxy resin to prevent air arcing between stages and steps the voltage up to ~250 kV peak across dual electrodes mounted on a 3D-printed rack-and-pinion mechanism, allowing me to adjust the spark gap distance mechanically without opening the chamber.

Hardware & Firmware Troubleshooting

ISSUE 01 // AIR ARCING ACROSS THE CAPACITOR-DIODE LADDER

When testing the bare 15-capacitor / 20-diode multiplier on a silicone mat, humid air (~3 kV/mm breakdown) allowed sparks to jump prematurely between stages. Fix: Potting the entire ladder in clear two-part epoxy resin (~20 kV/mm dielectric strength) stopped stage-to-stage arcing and reduced electromagnetic interference (EMI) that was resetting the microcontroller.

ISSUE 02 // ESP32 PIN LIMITS & I²C ADDRESS CONFLICTS

Connecting 9 sensors and 4 displays used almost every available pin on the 38-pin ESP32, and all three 0.96" SSD1306 OLED screens shared the same default 0x3C I²C address. Fix: Assigned GPIO 34–39 strictly as input-only sensor lines, separated the I²C buses, and used non-blocking millis() timers instead of delay() so the screens update smoothly while polling safety sensors.

ISSUE 03 // INVERTED LID INTERLOCK & FALSE OZONE READINGS

Fixed an inverted boolean in the infrared lid-interlock code that locked the 2.8" TFT screen in emergency mode, and corrected a miswired SC01-O3 analog line that was causing false 10 ppm readings in clean air.

MECHANICAL DESIGN & KINEMATICS

Parallel-Linkage Geometry & Base Actuation

Open-Source MK2

Unlike serial-link arms where motors sit on each moving joint and add extra weight to the arm, the EEZYbotARM MK2 mounts all three main MG995 metal-gear servos inside the stationary base. The dual parallel-linkage bars keep the gripper level with the table across its entire reach, reducing moving mass and improving stability during pick-and-place runs.

ACTUATORS

3× MG995 metal-gear servos (base, shoulder, reach) + 1× metal-gear micro-servo (gripper)

STRUCTURE

15+ 3D-printed PLA links, ball-bearing turntable base, and parallel linkage

CONTROLLER

Salvaged official Arduino Uno R3 with sensor shield running C++ serial control

WORKSPACE

~310 mm radial reach, 160° base rotation, 0–50 mm gripper opening

JOINT TOLERANCES & SERVO UPGRADE

3D-printed pivot holes shrank slightly during printing, so I reamed each bore by hand and secured the joints with M3/M4 locknuts and spacers. After stripping three plastic-gear micro-servos on the claw, I upgraded the gripper to a full metal-gear micro-servo.

SOFTWARE MOTION SMOOTHING

Commanding instant servo angle jumps caused high current spikes and shook the arm. In C++, I added step-by-step speed ramping so the servos accelerate and decelerate smoothly between target positions.

THERMOSYNC: Timetable-Based Classroom AC Control

~28% Peak Cooling Cut

In the UAE, air conditioning accounts for 60–70% of campus electricity, and lecture halls often stay cooled to 21°C all day even when empty. Installing motion sensors everywhere is expensive and unreliable—they often shut off while students sit quietly during exams or long lectures. Instead of buying hardware for every room, THERMOSYNC connects the school's existing class schedule database to the central Building Management System (BMS):

1. PRE-COOLING
T - 15 mins

Cools the room to 22°C about 15 minutes before class begins so it's comfortable when students walk in.

2. EMPTY SETBACK
26°C – 27°C

Raises the thermostat when no class is scheduled, cutting energy without letting the room get overly hot.

3. ZERO HARDWARE
Software Only

Runs entirely on the university's existing network controls—no new room sensors to buy or maintain.

3-Layer Technical Architecture:
